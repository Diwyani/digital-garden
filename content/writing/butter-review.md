*Butter: A Novel by Asako Yuzuki*

Have you come across a book about a woman accused of killing 3 men in the span of 6 months — all former lovers — that made you want to start cooking? No? I have.

There are two types of book reviews: one covers what happened — the plot, genre, themes, how it made you feel. The other explores how the book changed the compass within, the fabric of your mind, the fundamentals of your being. This is both. Without spoilers.

## The Book

Manako Kaji is the prominent character in *Butter* — a woman accused of killing three men within six months, all former lovers. She's said to have seduced lonely businessmen with her home cooking. The major point of discussion in the courtroom and media is around how a woman like Kaji, who is not conventionally pretty and is slightly overweight, could seduce three wealthy, elite men. (Ugh. Misogyny.)

The media questioned: why would a man, no matter how lonely, find her desirable?

Rika Machida, a 30-something journalist at Shumei Weekly, is intrigued by the case and thinks she can spin a different angle. She constantly tries to interview Kaji, but Kaji refuses to talk to any female journalist — until one day Rika ends her letter with:

> *P.S. I'm really curious about the recipe for the beef stew that you made for Mr. Yamamura. I'd be delighted if you'd share it with me.*

Conversation begins between two opposite characters. Kaji loves to cook elaborate meals, has multiple lovers, major commitment issues, is a food blogger, loves exquisite dining experiences, and is a huge material girl. Rika is a hardworking, passionate workaholic who barely has time to cook and relies on convenience store food or takeout.

During these conversations — meant to understand Kaji's psyche — Rika starts cooking elaborate meals with butter and gains weight. Each meal peels away a layer of Kaji's life, bringing fundamental changes to Rika's own, and eventually to mine.

## A Slow Read That Changes Everything

It's a slow read. It doesn't hold you in a chokehold or demand to be devoured in one sitting. I spent three and a half months with it, and it's been a year since I finished. It changed everything within.

I relate somewhat to Rika's life — the lack of time, barely eating properly. Kaji's adoration of elaborate recipes made me curious about cooking and eating well. I've taken out time and cooked several meals as an act of love.

Asako's *Butter* has a deceptive cover and description. It poses itself as a thriller, but the central theme is food — freshly cooked warm meals — and everything that stems from its presence and lack of it. There is isolation, loneliness, gender roles, body image issues, friendship, childhood wounds, self-care, and togetherness. Accepting oneself as they are. All of it stems from food.

There's a moment between Rika and her colleague Shinoi that stayed with me:

> **Rika:** It seems like you can cook, but you don't. It's a waste.
>
> **Shinoi:** If it's just for myself, I can't be bothered to cook good and healthy food.
>
> **Rika:** I think not taking care of yourself is an act of violence.

## What I Realized

Towards the end of those three and a half months, I realized: I didn't love myself enough. I was knee-deep in an act of violence. I relied heavily on the same meals or whatever I could fix quickly. Eating at all — let alone eating well — was always a secondary thought.

Deciding to cook, and being in the kitchen not knowing how to make things, still not looking up a recipe — just instinct and memories — has made me confident in my choices, comfortable with "not knowing" how it's going to turn out. I've deliciously adapted a learn-on-the-go mindset.

## What I've Learned from All the Cooking

Food is beyond calories and protein. Food is an exploration, a way back to memories. Warm meals are central to love, to the lack of love, to the lack of time, to the will to live. The book represents meals as a form of care, affection, and attention one can give to themselves and their loved ones. How having enough time to prepare it is a privilege limited to some.

Cooking is fun if it's an occasional event. But when the daily meal is a chore, it's either an unpaid job for one gender — said to have more nurturing instincts — or an underpaid job for the underprivileged. Neither is respected enough. That's a simple truth.

You need to cook for yourself all three meals at home for a month or so — to learn how your mother managed to cook before 7 in the morning, to respect others who work for you, to know that loving yourself is an everyday effort, for better companionship with your current or future partner, to know that the roles of nurturer and provider are fluid and you can take turns. Turns out you don't need a plot twist for character development. You can start with a mundane everyday task like cooking.

When you're done doing it for yourself, invite your friends for dinner. Know that you can cherish others only when you are overflowing with love. Life is suffering and our time here is limited. The least we can do is cook a warm meal for ourselves and the people we love — bring everyone together to hold space and time, which is almost always slipping.

---

> You need love. Hopefully it's love for the people you're cooking for, because the greatest and most memorable meals are as much about who you are with as they are about what you are. But love for what you're doing, and for the ingredients you're doing it with, will more than suffice. You must enjoy the fact that you are nourishing them, pleasing them, giving the best you've got.
>
> — Anthony Bourdain
