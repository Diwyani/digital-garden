*On choice paralysis, wasted potential, and tasting life anyway*

I drew this huge fig a year ago when I was contemplating all my choices: classic could've, should've. The urge to do everything, become everything. Inaction, choice paralysis, so many options — there is so much potential. Wasted potential. Nothing scares me more.

## The Context

In the early 1960s, Sylvia Plath published a book called *The Bell Jar* where she wrote the most gut-wrenching lines that have been haunting 20-somethings for years now. It's about being in your 20s and having all the possible directions life can go in front of you, being paralyzed by indecision, and having an "having it all" mindset.

Sylvia compares the possibility of the future with a fig tree:

> I saw my life branching out before me like the green fig tree in the story. From the tip of every branch, like a fat purple fig, a wonderful future beckoned and winked. One fig was a husband and a happy home and children, and another fig was a famous poet and another fig was a brilliant professor, and another fig was Ee Gee, the amazing editor, and another fig was Europe and Africa and South America…
>
> I saw myself sitting in the crotch of this fig tree, starving to death, just because I couldn't make up my mind which of the figs I would choose. I wanted each and every one of them, but choosing one meant losing all the rest, and, as I sat there, unable to decide, the figs began to wrinkle and go black, and, one by one, they plopped to the ground at my feet.
>
> — *The Bell Jar*, 1963

The Fig Tree problem is essentially a combination of the paradox of choice and decision paralysis that a person faces for the first time whenever they have earned the freedom to make choices on their own.

Once you reach here, there is no going back. You are choosing things daily — small mundane stuff (what to eat? what to watch? should we eat in or go out?) to big life-changing decisions (which career path to choose? should I move out of my hometown?).

I was hit by figs yet again a year ago. Looking for an answer, I overthought and over-discussed it with people older than me, hoping someone would hand me a roadmap, a step-by-step guide of what I'm supposed to do and who I'm supposed to be.

Days passed, the talk stopped, I read a lot. I kept finding words meant for me but sent to 19-year-old poet Franz Xaver Kappus by Rainer Maria Rilke in 1903:

> I want to beg you, as much as I can, dear sir, to be patient toward all that is unsolved in your heart and to try to love the questions themselves like locked rooms and like books that are written in a very foreign tongue. Do not now seek the answers, which cannot be given you because you would not be able to live them. And the point is, to live everything. Live the questions now. Perhaps you will then gradually, without noticing it, live along some distant day into the answer.

## Do We Have to Choose One Fig Before All of It Rots Away?

So I lived deliberately. Went for walks, cooked meals, and made habits that eased my restlessness of not knowing what's next — career, companionship, city. I thought about all these people who came before me, who had their own fig trees.

Years before David Lynch was a filmmaker, he was a painter. He says movies are just moving paintings, and painting led the way to this. Steve from *Stranger Things* is also the End of Beginning singer, DJo. Henri Matisse was a lawyer. Suzanne Jackson works experimentally across mediums, including drawing, painting, printmaking, bookmaking, poetry, dance, theater, and costume design.

There, we have one answer.

There's a popular phrase that's been tossed around on Twitter: **"You can just do things."** When they accomplish that — which clearly involved a lot of effort, like running 26 km in the beginning of 2026, coding, launching a whole new thing, learning a brand new skill — they put it simply with a glimmer of joy and pride. In that phrase, the "just" before doing is a placeholder for: stop thinking, stop talking, stop manifesting, stop planning.

## How Do We Start? What Do We Do?

Anne-Laure, writer of *Tiny Experiments*, might suggest picking your few figs and running a few experiments and side quests, collecting field notes. Now it's your turn to ask questions. What do you think about this fig? Is it fulfilling? How much effort does it take? Can you make time for it often? Can it be monetized? Will you keep putting in the work if it's not monetized?

If a fig is not worth it, throw it. Focus on what's left, and pick new ones. They say a jack of all trades is master of none. Well, I will be happier being master of none — having tasted so many figs, I will feast on my life and won't let those figs rot.

## Things to Keep in Mind

- Approach your fig with a sense of curiosity over fear.
- There are bigger forces at play that only communicate with your instincts.
- Being at your prime is a myth — every age is prime if you refuse to evolve.
- You can just do things.
